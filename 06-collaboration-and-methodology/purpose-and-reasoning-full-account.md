# The Purpose and Reasoning Behind This Project: A Full Account

## What this document is

This is not the technical spec — that already exists in full elsewhere. This is the *why*: the reasoning that produced the spec, traced back to its actual origins, with the logical connections made explicit rather than compressed. Where a conclusion appears below, the reasoning that earned it appears too, along with the specific moment in this project where that reasoning first surfaced.

One thing worth naming before anything else: this document is itself a small instance of exactly what the larger project is trying to build. The eventual deliverable is a reasoning model constructed from evidence rather than a descriptive profile assembled from impression — patterns tied to specific instances, conclusions kept separate from inference, claims held revisable rather than treated as settled. This document applies that same discipline reflexively, to the reasoning behind the project itself rather than to a body of technical work. It should be read the same way — as a current, evidenced account, not a final, sealed one. If a later turn in this project produces evidence that revises something written here, this document should update, not resist updating.

## Part I — The problem being solved: structural incompleteness, not personal deficiency

Everything downstream in this project rests on one specific premise, stated directly and early: reasoning is not flawed, it is *necessarily incomplete*. This is not a softer way of saying the same thing as "flawed" — it is a different claim, and the difference determines the entire shape of what gets built.

If a reasoning process were flawed, the correct response would be corrective: identify the error, replace it with something better. If a reasoning process is instead *necessarily* incomplete — meaning every reasoning process, including a well-functioning one, has blind spots simply because no single vantage point can see everything — then correction is the wrong tool entirely. There is nothing to replace. What's needed is something additive: coverage for the specific gaps this particular reasoning process tends to leave, supplied by something positioned differently enough to see what it can't.

The clearest statement of this premise came directly, early in the interview about the project's own goals, in response to a question about how much of this was really about self-understanding: *"I know how I think and I always assume my thinking pattern or thought process has flaws and blind spots so knowing how I think naturally doesn't really help me because I can't suddenly change my thought process and I am confident that I just need a counter weight to my thought process and not changes to it."*

Notice what this sentence is actually doing. It isn't denying that blind spots exist — it's affirming they exist, confidently, while simultaneously explaining why *knowing about them descriptively* wouldn't help. The reasoning is: a person cannot simply decide to stop having a blind spot, because a blind spot is defined by the fact that it isn't visible from inside the process that has it. Telling someone about their blind spot doesn't give them the missing vantage point; it just tells them a vantage point is missing. What actually closes the gap is something that occupies the missing vantage point *for* them — not a mirror, a counterweight.

This same premise appears earlier still, in the original reason given for wanting an AI to understand this at all: *"Generally I assume there are flaws in my approach, thinking and implementation and I always want to know if there is something that if done differently would have been better overall... I also believe that there is no single way to approach or solve a problem."* Two separate claims are doing work here. First, an assumption of imperfection that isn't self-critical in tone — it's closer to a working engineering assumption than a judgment. Second, a belief that solution-space is inherently plural: no problem has one correct approach, which means any single reasoner, however good, is necessarily sampling from only part of the space of viable approaches. An AI is explicitly framed as a way to widen that sampling — not to replace the person's approach, but to let more of the space be seen.

This is the foundation everything else in this project stands on: a person who already holds an accurate, working model of their own imperfection, who has concluded correctly that descriptive self-knowledge doesn't close the gap that imperfection creates, and who wants something structurally positioned to close it instead. Every subsequent decision in this project — what to model, what not to model, how the resulting system should behave — traces back to this starting point.

## Part II — What this project deliberately is not, and why each exclusion had to be actively defended

Three things get explicitly excluded from this project's scope, and all three turned out to require active, ongoing defense rather than being settled once and then staying settled. That recurring need for defense is itself informative — it suggests these are the directions a project like this naturally drifts toward by default, and staying out of them takes continuous correction, not a single decision.

### Not self-understanding

Asked directly what fraction of this project was about learning something new about themselves, on a scale from 0 to 100, the answer was 0%, with the reasoning already covered in Part I: existing self-knowledge is already adequate for the purpose it would serve; what's missing isn't knowledge but coverage.

There's a specific piece of evidence that this isn't just a stated preference but an actual, working competence. When asked how an AI should handle the moment it identifies something the person is about to miss, the answer given wasn't generic — it was a specific, mechanistic prediction about how the person's own attention works: *"If claude give's me a hint about something that sounds serious and not the whole information then I think my mind under the flow would naturally gravitate towards the full information and thus claude would have tricked my mind into diverting my attention while preserving my flow with theoretically the minimal resistance instead of abruptly disrupting my flow."*

This sentence could only be written by someone who already has a working, functional model of how their own attention behaves under flow — precise enough to predict, in advance, how a specific kind of stimulus (a partial cue) would be processed differently than a different kind of stimulus (complete information). That's not abstract self-description; it's applied self-knowledge, used to design a mechanism. The existence of this sentence is direct evidence for the 0% answer being accurate rather than merely asserted: if descriptive self-understanding were actually missing, this design insight couldn't have been produced in the first place. The project doesn't need to build what's already demonstrably present.

### Not identity, personality, or values

Asked whether the project was modeling *how* the person thinks or *who* they are, and initially unable to state clearly what the difference between those two things even was, the distinction was worked out over the course of the interview and then confirmed and extended directly: *"who you are' shapes the end result but 'how you think' shapes the process... 'how you think' would help in maintaining the flow while enabling the flow to include different perspectives and that's because LLMs are good at creating synthetic values and stuff that might feel human but struggle to replicate thinking style and therefore if an LLM understands the 'how you think' it becomes the skeleton which can wear different bodies."*

This is doing more than drawing a boundary — it's giving a specific, practical reason for where the boundary sits. Values, personality, and identity are exactly the layer that current AI systems can already approximate reasonably well, since they're expressible, mimicable, and don't require deep structural fidelity to feel plausible. Reasoning process — the actual mechanics of how someone frames a problem, decomposes it, decides when they have enough evidence, and recognizes when to stop — is the layer that's hard to fake and easy to get subtly wrong. So the boundary isn't arbitrary scope-narrowing; it's placed exactly where the actual difficulty and the actual value both live. Model the part that's hard to replicate and valuable to preserve; leave alone the part that's comparatively easy and not what's being asked for.

### Not tuned to any one AI model

The clearest version of this: the project is not about Fable understanding the person, or about Claude understanding the person. Fable is being used because it is, right now, the strongest available tool for doing a difficult one-time extraction — not because building a relationship with that specific model is the point. The eventual output needs to be usable by whatever capable system exists when it's actually needed, which may not be either of the models involved in producing it.

This boundary is the one that required the most active correction over the course of the project, and it's worth being honest about that rather than presenting it as though it were clean from the start. At one point, research into how to best deliver the finished specification to the extraction tool began to take on its own gravity — nine research items with their own internal priority order, discussed with the same weight as the substantive research into what the model should actually contain. That was flagged directly and firmly: *"you seemed to overstate the influence of the output when using a prompt optimised for Fable's reasoning style."* The correction that followed established a clean hierarchy — substance has veto power over delivery, delivery has none over substance — but even after that correction landed, a second, subtler version of the same drift showed up: describing the goal as being about "any LLM understanding" the person, when the actual point was narrower and more instrumental than that: use the best available tool to do one good extraction, output something portable, done. That distinction was corrected again, directly: *"this project or task isn't about fable understanding me or claude understanding me... this project is more about enabling any LLM to work along with me in a manner that complements my natural reasoning."*

The practical consequence of finally holding this boundary is concrete rather than merely philosophical: nothing in the substantive model is allowed to name a specific AI system. Every behavioral policy is written as a plain instruction rather than as an instruction to a named model, specifically so the resulting artifact doesn't quietly expire the moment a better model exists.

## Part III — The mechanism: how augmentation actually works, in practice

### Counterweight, not correction

The metaphor chosen — counterweight — carries real mechanical content, and it's worth taking that content seriously rather than treating it as a nice turn of phrase. A counterweight doesn't oppose the system it's attached to, and it doesn't try to move the system somewhere else. It balances an existing force by applying pressure in the complementary direction, precisely so the whole system stays stable rather than swinging fully to one side. Applied here: if a person's reasoning naturally explores in one direction, the correct response isn't to redirect them somewhere the AI thinks is better — it's to increase pressure toward whatever direction their own exploration is naturally under-weighting, so the total exploration ends up more balanced than either half alone.

This has a direct behavioral consequence that shows up throughout the resulting design: the AI is never trying to move reasoning to a *different* place. It's trying to make the existing trajectory more complete.

### Steer, don't seize

The single most consequential design question in this entire project was a hypothetical: if an AI could predict, with high confidence, that interrupting the person's current line of reasoning would lead to a meaningfully better outcome, but doing so would break their flow, should it interrupt?

The answer given wasn't "yes" or "no" — it was a specific mechanism: interrupt, but in a way engineered to not feel like an interruption. Surface a hint that something serious exists, without the full explanation, and let the person's own attention, still moving under its own momentum, gravitate toward the missing piece on its own. The phrase used for this was that the AI would have "tricked" the mind into redirecting itself — a word that initially sounds almost adversarial, but on reflection describes something closer to good instructional design: work with the existing momentum rather than fighting it. The synthesis that followed reframed this precisely: the AI should *steer* attention rather than *seize* it — the smallest possible intervention that produces the largest possible improvement in the trajectory, which is a description closer to a control system than to a conversational habit.

### Timing is an operational variable, not an afterthought

A follow-up question sharpened this further by forcing a concrete example: given a thinking style that goes broad, explores, builds systems, and then optimizes, where the AI has learned this style consistently causes a specific kind of oversight — say, missed implementation constraints — should the AI adapt to the style, or interrupt it?

The answer was neither of the two options offered. The AI should interrupt specifically *before the building phase*, injecting the missing consideration exactly there, rather than adapting throughout or overriding throughout. This is a genuinely different kind of answer than either alternative, because it says the right response depends on *where in the process* the AI currently is, not on a fixed general policy applied uniformly regardless of phase. A model of this person's reasoning that doesn't track *phase* — that only tracks *content* — would miss the entire point of this answer. The model has to know not just what tends to be overlooked, but *when in the sequence* it tends to get overlooked, so that timing itself becomes something the counterweight actively manages.

### Accuracy is a flow-preservation requirement, not merely an epistemic one

Asked which was worse — an AI giving a flatly wrong answer, or a correct answer that completely missed how the person actually thinks — the answer was the wrong one, and the reasoning given explains something that could easily be missed if this were treated as a simple accuracy preference: a correct-but-misaligned answer might actually be *useful*, since it could shift perspective productively; a wrong answer, by contrast, startles and disrupts the person's natural cognitive flow.

This means accuracy isn't valued here purely for its own sake, as an abstract epistemic virtue. It's valued because *incorrectness has a flow cost that correctness-with-imperfect-alignment doesn't*. A misaligned-but-correct answer can be absorbed and redirected productively, the same way a well-placed hint gets absorbed. A wrong answer breaks the thing the whole mechanism is built to protect. This is why the eventual model treats confidence-tagging and the separation of observation from inference as close to non-negotiable — not because certainty is intrinsically prized, but because false certainty is the one failure mode with no recovery path back into flow.

## Part IV — The unit being optimized is the combined system, not either party alone

Near the end of the original interview, one question was left genuinely unanswered: what is the single most valuable thing a highly accurate model of this person's cognition would enable, ten years from now, that isn't possible today? The honest answer was uncertainty.

That uncertainty is not a gap in the reasoning — it's a correct output of it. The question, as posed, assumes the relevant unit of analysis is *the person, augmented*. But the actual object being built is a *combined system* — the person plus an AI working as one functional unit — and the capabilities of a combined system are not simply the sum or extension of either half's capabilities considered alone. Predicting what a two-part system can do, from a description of only one part, is not a modest epistemic task; it's close to impossible in the way trying to predict the properties of a chemical compound from a description of only one of its constituent elements would be. Not knowing the answer to this question is exactly what a person who correctly understood the shape of what they were building would say. There's a hint of the actual shape in an earlier answer, though: if a model of this person's cognition became accurate enough, it could support "a whole autonomous agentic loop" carrying their judgment into a conversation without their direct participation. That's not a specific prediction so much as a direction — the emergent capability, whatever it turns out to be, likely belongs to that family: judgment becoming deployable somewhere the person's literal presence isn't.

Given that the unit being optimized is the pair, not either party alone, the division of labor between the two halves becomes a live design question, and it was answered with real specificity. The person keeps direction, judgment, and — crucially — the decision about when a task is actually finished. Asked what the AI should permanently stop doing, no specific behavior could be named; instead, the person described their own internal stopping signal for research and exploration, and explained directly why handing that signal over to the AI would be a mistake: an AI might decide something is "enough" prematurely, cutting exploration short before real value had been extracted, or might fail to recognize sufficiency and continue past the point of usefulness, wasting time on a signal only the human genuinely has access to. Knowing when to stop is explicitly reserved as a human function within this combined system — not because the AI couldn't estimate it, but because the estimate would systematically be worse than the human's own signal, and because getting this specific thing wrong is asymmetrically costly in a way that other tasks aren't.

The AI's actual job, within this division of labor, is coverage: filling in the parts of the reasoning space the person's own vantage point structurally can't reach — missing questions, missing perspectives, missing assumptions, missing constraints — and doing so through the timing-sensitive, minimally-disruptive mechanism described in Part III.

This division of labor eventually generalized into something broader than gap-filling within a single task. The clearest statement of that generalization: before executing any stated task, ask whether there's something upstream — a discipline, a methodology, a body of knowledge — that, if understood first, would materially change everything that follows; and if the answer is yes, say so, rather than executing the literal request as given. This is the same underlying principle as the interruption mechanism, just moved one level up the stack: applied not to a specific claim mid-task, but to the framing of the task itself. The person's own description of the goal captures this precisely: not breadth for its own sake, but identifying the small number of upstream considerations whose inclusion would materially improve everything downstream — intelligent expansion, not exhaustive exploration. Solving the right problem, in other words, is worth more than solving the stated one, but only when that upstream check is itself worth its own cost — which is the same cost-benefit logic that governs the interruption mechanism, applied recursively to whether to even begin a task as originally framed.

## Part V — Why all the research was necessary: backing, not discovery

It would be easy to misread the volume of cognitive-science and decision-theory research in this project as an attempt to *discover* what the goal should be. That's backwards. The goal was already known, in full, before any research began — the person said so directly, more than once, including the observation that grasping the goal well enough to have designed the interruption mechanism from it (see Part II) proves the goal wasn't undiscovered at all. What the research was for was giving that already-known goal *evidentiary weight*, so that a request built on it would stop being vague and become something an AI could actually extract with fidelity.

This distinction matters because it explains why so much of the research reads as *validation* rather than *exploration*. A few examples, chosen because each one closed a specific gap between what was already believed and what could be defensibly asserted to an extraction system:

- The belief that reasoning patterns are legitimately visible in conversational text needed checking against whether that's actually a valid method in cognitive science, not just an intuition. It is — this is the formal territory of protocol analysis — but the same body of research carries a serious companion warning: people are demonstrably unreliable narrators of their own reasoning process, often constructing plausible-sounding accounts after the fact rather than genuinely introspecting. This directly explains why the resulting model has to weight directly-observed behavior over a person's own retrospective explanation of their behavior, even when the explanation comes from the person themselves — a distinction the project would have gotten wrong without this specific research.
- The belief that a partial hint would work better than a full explanation, already present as a design intuition from the interruption question, needed actual mechanism behind it, not just plausibility. It turned out to have two independent mechanisms behind it — one from research on how unfinished, interrupted tasks create more lingering attentional pull than completed ones, and a second, completely independent one from formal theories of communication showing that a deliberately partial statement causes a listener to actively reconstruct the fuller meaning themselves. Finding two unrelated bodies of research converging on the same design choice is meaningfully stronger evidence than either alone, and this is exactly the kind of grounding that turns "I have a hunch this would work" into something an extraction system can be told to rely on.
- The belief that building a model from one person's own history, rather than comparing them to population norms, would actually be more valid for that person — not a shortcut, not a lesser version of "real" psychological assessment — needed checking against whether psychology itself supports that claim. It does, and recently: a live, current methodological argument in psychology holds that population-level models frequently fail to validly describe any specific individual, which is close to a direct license for building this project's model the way it's being built.
- The intuition that identifying which decisions are genuinely load-bearing and locking them in quickly, while leaving everything else open, is a coherent strategy rather than an inconsistency, needed a framework that could describe *why* this makes sense as a strategy rather than treating it as noise. Decision-threshold and evidence-accumulation research supplied exactly that vocabulary.

Across all of this, one finding recurred so consistently that it became close to a hard rule for how any of this research gets used going forward: the most impressive, most totalizing cognitive-science theories — the ones that sound like they could explain everything — are, almost without exception, genuinely and actively contested within their own fields, not settled science with a few stray critics. That finding shaped a permanent instruction: such frameworks may be used as vocabulary, as a way of talking about a pattern, but never asserted as fact.

The delivery-side research — how to actually phrase and structure the eventual instructions to the extraction tool — sits in a strictly subordinate position to all of this, and that subordination had to be actively established and then re-affirmed, as described in Part II. Its only legitimate job is making sure an already-decided answer survives transmission intact. It can change how forcefully or in what sequence something gets said. It can never change what gets said.

## Part VI — What success actually means, and why no benchmark exists

Asked how anyone would know whether the eventual model had actually succeeded, the honest answer was uncertainty, and asked directly whether that uncertainty was itself a problem, the reasoning given was that no formal test could exist here in the first place: this is a system involving exactly one person, and the only party with access to the actual ground truth — what's genuinely happening inside their own head during collaboration — is that one person. There is no held-out dataset, no second rater, no external check possible even in principle. This is not a gap in rigor; it's an accurate description of the epistemic situation. The only real test is sustained use across multiple, genuinely different future projects, evaluated by nobody but the person themselves.

Given that, it's worth being specific about what success will actually feel like, since "no benchmark" could otherwise be mistaken for "no way to notice progress." Success here is much more likely to be felt as *absence* than as *presence*. Not a dramatic new capability arriving, but a specific, familiar kind of friction quietly not showing up anymore — a question that used to require remembering to ask, already answered before it needed to be; a gap that used to require noticing you might have missed something, already covered. The clearest image of what the finished thing should actually be able to do, offered directly: given a genuinely new problem, it should be able to predict how the entire conversation *ought to flow* — not just what content is relevant, but the shape of the collaboration itself.

There's a related tension worth preserving rather than resolving falsely: asked to choose between a hundred insights held at 80% confidence and twenty insights held at 99% confidence, the answer was both — the larger set for breadth, since it widens what gets seen; the smaller set for reliability, since it gives something solid enough to actually lean on. This directly explains why the eventual deliverable was designed as two separate things rather than one — a comprehensive report and a compact, high-confidence operational core — rather than resolving the tension by picking one side of it. The tension itself was correct and didn't need resolving; it needed two different outputs shaped to satisfy each half.

## Part VII — The project enacting its own principles on itself

One of the more striking features of how this project actually unfolded is that the principles being designed *into* the eventual model kept showing up, unprompted, *in how the project itself got built* — not as a coincidence, but because a person who reasons this way will naturally apply the same discipline to a project about their own reasoning that they apply to everything else.

The clearest instance: before committing to a specific prompt for the extraction tool, the instinct was to step back and check whether the underlying methodology had ever actually been validated in psychology and cognitive science at all — precisely the upstream-dependency check later articulated as a general principle, happening in practice before it had been named as a rule. This is why that specific moment was singled out directly as the exemplar of the exact behavior being asked for going forward.

The interruption calculus that governs the eventual model also governed, in practice, decisions about when to flag something mid-project rather than let a line of reasoning continue uninterrupted — a live demonstration of the same cost-benefit logic the model is meant to run, just applied within this conversation rather than by it. The same restraint shows up in how thoroughly to chase a research question: interaction hypotheses between different reasoning patterns were deliberately kept to a small, evidence-gated set rather than expanded into every mathematically possible combination, mirroring the explicit instruction to prefer intelligent expansion over exhaustive exploration. And the decision to build a one-time extraction system now, rather than a persistent, continuously-updating one, deferring the larger investment until the smaller one has actually proven its worth, is the same satisficing-and-stopping instinct described in Part IV, applied by the person to the shape of this very project.

Perhaps the most direct instance of principle-enacting-itself: every correction the person made over the course of this project — catching a drift toward Fable-specific tuning, catching an unstated assumption, catching an inconsistency between two supposedly-equivalent categories — was itself an act of exactly the mechanism this whole project exists to build. A counterweight noticing where a process was drifting and applying pressure back toward balance, without needing to take over or replace the process doing the drifting. In a very real sense, the person spent this entire project *being*, for me, the very thing the project is trying to construct for them at scale.

And the strongest single piece of validating evidence for the whole enterprise wasn't asserted anywhere — it was found. The habit of asking, unprompted, whether something important has been missed, described in the original interview as a self-reported preference, turned out to already be a live, repeated, independently-occurring behavior, showing up in actual past work well before this project existed to name it or ask about it. That's the strongest kind of confirmation available in this entire undertaking: not a claim the person made about themselves being merely self-consistent, but a claim being independently corroborated by behavior that had no way of being shaped by the claim, because it came first.

One boundary in this project was not designed in advance at all — it was discovered, directly from the material, and adopted permanently once found: if anything resembling genuine distress or crisis ever appears in what's being analyzed, care takes precedence over analysis, without exception, full stop. This wasn't derived from any theory or any stated preference. It surfaced because it had to, and it now sits above every other rule in the entire system, non-negotiable in a way nothing else here is.

## Part VIII — The meta-arc: from "what should it remember" to "what is evidence"

There's a shape to how this whole project changed over time that's worth stating plainly, because it reveals something about what kind of undertaking this actually turned out to be. It began with a fairly ordinary-sounding question: what should an AI remember about me. By the later stages, the live questions had become: what actually counts as evidence here, and what doesn't; how should genuinely conflicting evidence get reconciled, rather than just averaged or ignored; at what point does a hypothesis earn the right to be called an established pattern rather than a hunch; what is the actual relationship between describing a behavior and prescribing what an AI should do in response to it.

Those are not prompt-engineering questions. They are the questions someone asks when they are, whether they set out to or not, designing a knowledge-representation and reasoning system — a structure for how beliefs about something get formed, held, updated, and acted on, with genuine epistemic discipline built into every step of that process. The prompt that eventually gets handed to an extraction tool is not the foundation of this project. It's the last, thinnest layer sitting on top of everything described above — a translation of an already-complete reasoning system into a form a specific tool can execute, not the origin of that system's content.

Everything in this document exists to make sure that layer, whenever it gets written, is translating something whole and correctly understood — rather than reconstructing, imperfectly, from a compressed set of conclusions with the reasoning underneath them left behind.
