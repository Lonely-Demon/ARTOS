# Mathematical & Theoretical Cognitive Science Frameworks for an Idiographic Reasoning Model — Integrated Report

*This document merges two independently-conducted research passes on the same question: which mathematical and theoretical cognitive-science frameworks could ground a text-derived, individual model of one person's reasoning, for an AI collaborator designed to act as a counterweight rather than a mirror. Where both passes converged on the same framework without coordinating, that convergence is called out explicitly — independent agreement is itself a signal of how load-bearing a framework is for this project. Where one pass found something the other missed, it's folded in at full depth.*

## Executive Summary

Two independent passes converged, without coordination, on a strikingly similar shortlist of genuinely usable frameworks: evidence-accumulation models of decision-making, model-based/model-free reinforcement learning, formal metacognition (signal detection theory and meta-d′), resource-rational analysis, information-foraging and curiosity models, and Rational Speech Act theory for communication design. That convergence is worth trusting more than either pass alone.

Each pass also found something distinctive the other missed entirely. The first pass's standout contribution is Personal Construct Theory and the Repertory Grid technique — the one concrete, validated methodology in this whole landscape purpose-built to elicit a specific individual's own idiosyncratic construct system — plus a hard empirical reality check (Park et al.'s 2024 generative-agent study, the Centaur foundation model, and the emerging "digital twin" validity critique literature) on whether any of this can actually be extracted from free text. The second pass's standout contribution is computational psychiatry's Hierarchical Gaussian Filter (Mathys et al., 2011) — arguably the single closest existing technique to this project's literal goal, since it's explicitly built to quantify one specific person's learning rate and volatility-handling from their behavior — plus clean, decision-theoretic answers to the two concrete open design questions this project keeps circling back to: the Expected Value of Control framework for when to interrupt, and Marginal Value Theorem / optimal-stopping theory for when to abandon a line of inquiry.

The single most important shared conclusion, reached independently by both passes, is a caution rather than a discovery: almost every powerful unifying theory here — the Bayesian brain, predictive processing and active inference, strict two-system dual-process theory, the dynamical-systems hypothesis — is actively and seriously contested in its own field, not settled fact. Both passes concluded, on their own, that the master prompt must treat these as lenses with named boundary conditions, never as facts the AI can assert. That double confirmation is probably the single highest-value output of having run two passes at all.

## Key Findings

**1. The project needs two different kinds of framework, and conflating them is the main risk.** One kind gives a small number of interpretable, individually-varying parameters — a decision threshold, a learning rate, a metacognitive efficiency score — describable about a specific person even where they can't be numerically fitted from text. The other kind gives vocabulary and justification — a way to talk about why the whole project is legitimate, or why an intervention should look a certain way — without itself producing a parameter. Treating the second kind as if it produces the first is where most of the contested, unfalsifiable territory lives (the Bayesian brain, active inference).

**2. The two open design questions this project keeps returning to — when to interrupt, when to help the person recognize a search has run its course — both turn out to have existing, formal, decision-theoretic answers.** The second pass surfaced these independently: Expected Value of Control for the interruption question, Marginal Value Theorem plus optimal-stopping theory for the abandonment question. Both replace a gut feeling with a stated, tunable rule, and both come with an important twist — optimal-stopping research finds people generally quit searching *too early*, not too late, meaning the counterweight's job may often be to push for more exploration, not less.

**3. Computational psychiatry's Hierarchical Gaussian Filter is the closest thing that exists to the project's literal ambition** — a validated method whose entire purpose is fitting one person's belief-updating style (how fast they revise, how much volatility they assume, how they weight prior belief against new evidence) from their own behavior. It should organize the belief-and-inference side of the model, with drift-diffusion (decision commitment), reinforcement learning (planning vs. habit), and meta-d′ (confidence calibration) as complementary parameters covering what HGF doesn't.

**4. Personal Construct Theory's Repertory Grid technique is the one existing method built to elicit an individual's own idiosyncratic categories**, rather than scoring them against a pre-built inventory — a natural fit for an idiographic project, and the clearest concrete elicitation method available for actually building the model from the transcripts, rather than just describing what such a model would contain.

**5. Both passes, independently, landed on Rational Speech Act theory as the formal account of why "a hint, not the full concern" should work.** RSA's recursive speaker-listener model predicts that a deliberately partial, informationally incomplete utterance causes the listener to actively reconstruct the fuller intended meaning themselves — a second, independent justification (alongside the first research pass's attention-residue/Zeigarnik-effect finding) for the same design choice.

**6. Functionalism/multiple realizability and Marr's three levels of analysis are two halves of one justification** for the project's entire framing: Marr specifies which level to model at (computational/algorithmic, not implementational); functionalism explains why that's coherent and respectable at all (what's preserved across different "bodies" is the functional-causal role, not the substrate). Together, a rigorous version of "the skeleton that wears different bodies."

**7. Nearly every grand unifying theory here is genuinely, actively contested in its own field** — not settled consensus with a few critics, but a live, ongoing dispute. This applies to the Bayesian brain, predictive processing/active inference, strict dual-process theory, the dynamical-systems hypothesis, and even Hong & Page's "cognitive diversity beats ability" theorem, which has a documented mathematical rebuttal. The master prompt needs to say this outright, or the AI risks stating any of these as fact.

**8. The honest ceiling on all of this is empirical, not theoretical.** The strongest published result on modeling a specific person from their own text (Park et al., 2024, using two-hour interview transcripts as LLM context for 1,052 real people) replicates that person's own survey answers about 85% as well as the person replicates their own answers two weeks later — validating the basic approach — but performs far worse on strategic and economic decisions, and a growing "digital twin" validity literature documents real instability ("measurement phantoms") in text-derived individual models. No parameter discussed anywhere in this report — drift rate, learning rate, meta-d′, precision-weighting — can currently be numerically fitted from free conversational text; every one must be treated as a qualitative, revisable hypothesis.

## Details

### I. Setting the Frame — What Kind of Model This Is

**Marr's three levels of analysis.** David Marr (1982) distinguished the computational level (what problem is being solved, and why), the algorithmic/representational level (what representations and procedures are used), and the implementational level (the physical substrate). This is conceptual rather than mathematical, but it's the single most useful early decision for this project: declare the cognitive-synchronization model computational-and-algorithmic, explicitly disclaiming any implementational (neural) pretension. There's live debate — a 2015 *Topics in Cognitive Science* retrospective marking thirty years since Marr, and Love's (2015) commentary — about whether the algorithmic level is being squeezed out from below by neuroscience and from above by Bayesian analysis, which is reason enough to pick the level deliberately rather than let it drift.

**Functionalism and multiple realizability.** Putnam's Turing-machine functionalism and Fodor's related work hold that mental states are individuated by their functional-causal role, not by the physical stuff realizing them: a state counts as a given belief because of what causes it and what it causes, regardless of substrate, so the same functional profile is multiply realizable across different physical systems. This maps with unusual precision onto the "skeleton that wears different bodies" framing already in the project — it's the formal philosophical argument for exactly that move, licensing the project's central abstraction of modeling *how* the person reasons (functional role) while deliberately scoping out *who they are* (physical or personal substrate). One nuance worth carrying into the prompt: Putnam himself later turned multiple realizability against strong functionalism, so this should be presented as a useful, respectable scaffold rather than an uncontested truth. Read together, Marr and functionalism form a complete justification: Marr says which level to build at, functionalism says why building at that level — rather than modeling identity or substrate — is intellectually coherent.

**Cybernetics, Perceptual Control Theory, and the Good Regulator Theorem.** William Powers's Perceptual Control Theory (1973) holds that organisms control their own *perceptions*, not their behavior — behavior varies as needed to keep a perceived variable at an internally-set reference value, via a negative-feedback loop closed through the environment, with circular rather than linear causation; Powers proposed roughly eleven nested levels of such control. Carver and Scheier's (1981) cybernetic self-regulation applies the same feedback logic to everyday goal pursuit. The formal capstone is the Good Regulator Theorem (Conant & Ashby, 1970), which proves that any regulator that is simultaneously maximally successful and maximally simple must effectively contain a model of the system it regulates — building an internal model of what you're trying to regulate isn't an optional nicety but a mathematical requirement for good regulation. This matches the project's own language unusually well: the user independently reached for control-systems terms ("counterweight," "balancing exploration") before any of this research was done. Powers and Carver/Scheier let that instinct be formalized literally rather than left as metaphor, and Conant & Ashby give a rigorous reason the whole modeling project needs to exist at all — a counterweight AI must contain a model of the person specifically because that's what being an effective regulator requires.

### II. Decision-Making, Risk & Cognitive Control

**Evidence-accumulation models (drift-diffusion, linear ballistic accumulator).** Ratcliff's diffusion decision model (1978) treats a two-choice decision as noisy evidence accumulating from a starting point toward one of two boundaries, governed chiefly by drift rate (the efficiency of evidence extraction), boundary separation (how much evidence is demanded before committing — a speed-accuracy or "caution" setting), starting-point bias, and non-decision time. The framework is mature, neurally grounded, and reviewed extensively (Ratcliff, Smith, Brown & McKoon, 2016), though debates continue over collapsing versus fixed decision boundaries, with some collapsing-threshold variants showing poor parameter recovery in recent (2025) work. Individual-differences research (Weigard, Molloy, Sripada & Heathcote, 2026, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*) finds drift rate behaves as a coherent factor across many different tasks for a given person, tracking general cognitive ability and working memory, and reflects the *efficiency* rather than raw *speed* of evidence accumulation; boundary separation indexes a person's characteristic caution setting. For the project: model the person as having a signature boundary separation — do they commit on thin evidence, or demand a great deal before deciding? The counterweight's job is to notice when that personal threshold is miscalibrated for the actual stakes in front of them (too low risks premature commitment, too high risks paralysis) and cue accordingly.

**Resource-rational analysis, satisficing, fast-and-frugal heuristics, and the maximizer–satisficer distinction.** Herbert Simon (1955, 1956) originated bounded rationality: because the human mind's capacity for solving complex problems is small relative to the complexity of real-world problems, people satisfice — search sequentially and stop at the first option clearing a personal aspiration level — rather than optimize across every alternative. Lieder and Griffiths (2020, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) reframe this more formally as resource-rational analysis: heuristics as the optimal use of scarce computational resources rather than as errors, via a five-step method (specify the ideal computational solution, posit feasible bounded algorithms and their costs, derive the algorithm that optimally trades accuracy against cost, generate predictions, iterate). A closely related, more concretely specified tradition is Gigerenzer's ecological rationality and the "adaptive toolbox" of fast-and-frugal heuristics — simple, precisely specified procedures like take-the-best (decide using the single most valid discriminating cue) or the recognition heuristic, which can match or outperform exhaustive comparison exactly when they exploit real structure in the environment. Resource-rational analysis is influential but admittedly informal in its published presentation, and drew substantive critical commentary questioning whether resource constraints alone can rescue Bayesian accounts from behavior that looks straightforwardly anti-Bayesian. A directly relevant individual-differences measure sits alongside all of this: Schwartz and colleagues' (2002, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) maximizer–satisficer distinction, built around a Maximization Scale, found that people who tend to maximize — exhaustively comparing every option — reported systematically lower happiness, optimism, and self-esteem, and more depression, perfectionism, and regret, than people who tend to satisfice, though the scale's own psychometrics have since been questioned. For the project: treat the person's apparent heuristics and shortcuts as adaptations to their real constraints, not defects to be corrected by default; model where they sit on the maximizing-satisficing spectrum per project type, given that spectrum's known wellbeing correlates.

**Model-based vs. model-free reinforcement learning.** Daw, Niv and Dayan's (2005) dual-controller account distinguishes model-free control — cached stimulus-response values built up through trial and error, cheap and habitual but inflexible — from model-based control, which uses an internal model of the environment to compute values on the fly, flexible but computationally expensive. The live open problem is arbitration between the two systems: Lee, Shimojo and O'Doherty (2014, *Neuron*) linked reliability- and uncertainty-based arbitration to ventrolateral and inferior prefrontal cortex activity, and later work shows task complexity interacts with that uncertainty signal. This arbitration can misfire in individually diagnostic ways: Ruan and colleagues (2023, *Frontiers in Psychiatry*), testing 30 people with OCD against 120 controls on a three-stage Markov decision task, found the OCD group relied more heavily on model-free strategies specifically in conditions where model-based planning was the better approach (p = 0.012) — evidence of an impaired arbitration mechanism rather than a general deficit in either system alone. For the project: model whether the person defaults to cached, habitual moves when a changed situation actually calls for explicit re-planning, or the reverse — over-planning when a practiced habit would serve fine. The counterweight's job is to flag a likely arbitration mismatch, not to prefer one mode over the other in general.

**Formal risk and tradeoff theory: prospect theory and expected utility.** Expected utility theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern) is the normative baseline — choose to maximize probability-weighted utility. Prospect theory and its later cumulative refinement (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992) is the dominant descriptive alternative, built on a value function defined over gains and losses relative to a reference point (concave for gains, convex for losses), loss aversion (losses loom larger than equivalent gains), and probability weighting (small probabilities overweighted, large ones underweighted). A 2025 paper (Kim, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*) estimates distinct value functions tied to individual decision-making style, and multi-attribute utility theory extends the framework to tradeoffs spanning several dimensions at once. For the project: moderately-to-highly useful for describing the person's risk and tradeoff reasoning — how loss-averse they seem, how they weight low-probability risks, where their reference point tends to sit — describable qualitatively from how they frame decisions in text, with the standard caveat that these parameters are normally fit from monetary-gamble experiments, so naturalistic estimation here is approximate.

### III. The Two Open Design Questions, Formalized

This project has circled two concrete open questions since early on: when the AI should interrupt, and when it should help the person recognize a line of inquiry has run its course. Both turn out to have existing formal answers.

**When to interrupt: Expected Value of Control.** Shenhav, Botvinick and Cohen (2013, *Neuron*) proposed a normative model integrating three factors into a single control-allocation decision: the expected payoff of engaging a controlled process, the amount of control needed to achieve that payoff, and the intrinsic cost of exerting that control. Control, on this account, is expensive and recruited only when it clears a net-benefit bar — a framework since integrated with motivational-intensity theory (Silvestrini et al., 2023) and extended in more recent value-maximization work. This gives the interruption question a literal, quantifiable-in-principle rule: issue a cue only when the expected value of redirecting the person's attention exceeds the estimated disruption cost of doing so. It also gives formal backing to a design choice already settled earlier in this project — a minimal hint carries a much lower disruption cost than a full explanation, so it clears the EVC bar far more often, which is precisely why "hint, not the full concern" should outperform "interrupt with everything" as a default policy.

**When to abandon a line of inquiry: Marginal Value Theorem and optimal stopping.** Pirolli and Card's (1999) information foraging theory adapts Charnov's (1976) Marginal Value Theorem from behavioral ecology: a forager should leave its current patch once the patch's instantaneous rate of return drops to the environment's average rate. This generalizes well beyond literal foraging — Turrin, Fagan, Dal Monte and Chang (2017, *Scientific Reports*) found the same principle held for rhesus macaques seeking social information, with patch-leaving behavior best explained by an MVT-style model against three competing alternatives. A second, independent formalization of the same question comes from classical optimal-stopping theory — the "secretary problem," whose canonical solution is to observe roughly the first 37% (1/e) of options to calibrate expectations, then commit to the first one that beats that benchmark. Behavioral work here delivers an important twist: Ten and colleagues (2024, *Communications Psychology*) found people consistently search less than the optimal stopping rule prescribes, driven by overly pessimistic expectations about what later options will look like — humans, in other words, tend to stop too early rather than too late. For the project: give the AI both a rate-based stopping heuristic (track marginal insight per unit effort against the person's typical cross-project yield, and flag diminishing returns) and this behavioral correction — because people generally under-search, the counterweight should be at least as ready to cue continued exploration as to cue abandonment, rather than defaulting to "help them stop sooner."

### IV. Belief-Updating, Uncertainty & Confidence

**The Bayesian brain, predictive processing, and active inference — contested vocabulary.** The Bayesian-brain hypothesis (surveyed in Tenenbaum, Kemp, Griffiths & Goodman, 2011, *Science*) treats cognition as probabilistic inference over hierarchically structured mental models that describe the causal structure believed to generate one's observations. Predictive processing (Clark's 2013 "Whatever next?"; Hohwy's *The Predictive Mind*) and Friston's active-inference/free-energy program extend this into a full architecture: hierarchical generative models issue top-down predictions, precision-weighted prediction errors propagate upward and drive belief revision, and action becomes a way of reducing prediction error by changing the input rather than the model. All of this is intellectually powerful, and all of it is seriously, actively contested — not settled consensus with minor critics. A 2023 critique in the *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* argues the dominant strand of Bayesian cognitive science is under-constrained; a 2025 critique argues the Bayesian-brain framework is vulnerable to post-hoc adjustment of priors and likelihoods (an unfalsifiability problem), faces real computational-intractability concerns for exact inference, and lacks clear biological grounding; predictive processing draws similar unfalsifiability criticism — the same framework can be stretched to explain both excessive and insufficient belief-updating, as in competing accounts of schizophrenia — plus the well-known "dark room problem" and continued disputes over identifiable neural mechanisms. For the project: adopt the vocabulary of priors, evidence, and precision-weighted updating as the backbone of the "separate observation from inference, tag confidence" requirement, but require the AI to state what observation would actually change a given inference, guarding directly against the unfalsifiability trap these critiques describe.

**Computational psychiatry and the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter.** Mathys, Daunizeau, Friston and Stephan's (2011, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*) Hierarchical Gaussian Filter models an agent as inverting a generative model in which hidden states evolve as coupled random walks, with higher levels of the hierarchy encoding the volatility of the levels below; the resulting update equations move beliefs in proportion to precision-weighted prediction errors, and the effective learning rate adapts automatically to the estimated volatility of the environment. This is well-established within computational psychiatry, still being actively extended — a generalized HGF adding value/noise coupling appeared in 2025, alongside ongoing robustness work — and its explicit purpose is to quantify one specific person's individual differences in learning and inference from their behavior. This is arguably the single closest existing technique to this project's literal ambition, and should function as the organizing spine of the belief-and-inference side of the model: how fast does this person update given new evidence; do they treat a given domain as stable or volatile, and is that assumption well-calibrated to how the domain actually behaves; how do they weight prior belief against new information. The counterweight's job, in HGF terms, is to notice where the person's volatility estimate for a domain looks miscalibrated against how that domain is actually behaving — over-reacting to noise as if it were signal, or under-reacting to a genuine regime change.

**Formal metacognition: signal detection theory and meta-d′.** Signal detection theory separates sensitivity (d′ — how well someone discriminates signal from noise) from response bias or criterion (c — their threshold for committing to a judgment). Maniscalco and Lau (2012, *Consciousness and Cognition*) extended this into metacognition specifically with meta-d′ — the sensitivity an ideal observer would need to produce the confidence-accuracy relationship actually observed — and the ratio meta-d′/d′, sometimes called metacognitive efficiency or the M-ratio, normalizes this for raw task performance, with a ratio of 1 indicating optimal self-monitoring and lower ratios indicating under-monitoring. Fleming's (2017) HMeta-d adds a hierarchical Bayesian estimator that stays robust with limited data. This toolkit is now standard, and is even being extended to evaluate LLM confidence calibration in very recent (2026) work; a live theoretical debate persists over whether confidence is best explained as a separate second-order readout (a dual-process view) or simply a graded property of the same first-order decision process (a single-process view). For the project: this is the direct mathematical backbone of the "tag confidence, separate observation from inference" requirement — the AI should track its own sensitivity (does its stated confidence track its actual accuracy?) separately from its bias (is it systematically over- or under-confident?), and should model the person's calibration the same way, flagging the specific domains where they run consistently over- or under-confident.

**Dual-process theory, formalized.** Evans and Stanovich's (2013, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) default-interventionist architecture holds that fast, autonomous Type 1 processing generates a default response unless slower, working-memory-dependent Type 2 reasoning intervenes to override it. This remains genuinely contested: single-process accounts argue the same phenomena arise from graded properties of one underlying system rather than two literally distinct ones, and even Evans and Stanovich concede that several supposed dual-process features are only loosely correlated rather than a clean natural kind. For the project: use Type 1/Type 2 as a loose, useful descriptive scaffold for when the person tends to engage deliberate override versus run on intuition — but frame it as more-versus-less reflective processing along a gradient, not as two separate literal systems, given how seriously the single-process critique undercuts the strong version of the claim.

### V. The Wider Theoretical Landscape

**Cognitive architectures: ACT-R, Soar, CLARION.** Anderson's ACT-R and Newell's Soar are full computational simulations of cognition integrating working memory, procedural production rules, and long-term declarative memory; CLARION (Sun) adds an explicit split between a symbolic, explicit top level and a subsymbolic, implicit bottom level — a natural template for modeling explicit reasoning versus intuition specifically. Laird's (2022) detailed comparison of ACT-R and Soar highlights real architectural differences even as both now feed into a shared "Common Model of Cognition"; both architectures are recognized to underrepresent emotion, embodiment, and rich social context. For the project: too heavyweight to build in full — attempting a genuine ACT-R-level model of one person from conversation transcripts would be impractical and would over-commit to mechanism — but valuable as a completeness checklist for what a coherent computational model of someone's cognition needs to at least gesture at (declarative knowledge, procedural skill, memory activation, confidence/utility metadata), while explicitly scoping the actual prompt to the reasoning-relevant subset and naming what's deliberately left out.

**Dynamical systems theory.** Van Gelder's (1998, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) "dynamical hypothesis" frames cognitive agents as dynamical systems whose cognition is the continuous evolution of a state through a space, using the centrifugal governor rather than the digital computer as the paradigm case; concepts like attractor states, trajectories, and phase transitions replace discrete symbol manipulation. This has been self-critiqued as under-specified — commentators argued it collapses into connectionism in different clothing, and questioned whether instantiating a dynamical system is either necessary or sufficient for something to count as cognition, with a 2024 retrospective in *Topics in Cognitive Science* revisiting the debate more than two decades on. For the project: this supplies exactly the right formal vocabulary for the "steer, don't seize" attention design goal already established — flow becomes an attractor state, a full interruption becomes a large perturbation risking a jump into a different attractor (breaking flow entirely), and a well-placed minimal hint becomes a small perturbation that nudges the trajectory without dislodging it from its basin. Useful and evocative, but flagged as heuristic rather than literally established mechanism.

**4E cognition and enactivism.** The broader 4E umbrella — embodied, embedded, enacted, extended — situates the already-adopted extended-mind thesis within a wider tradition; enactivism specifically (Varela, Maturana, Thompson's *The Embodied Mind*) holds that a self-producing, autonomous agent brings forth meaning through sensorimotor coupling with its environment, rather than representing a pre-given world internally. For the project: this is the deeper theoretical home for treating the human-AI pairing as a genuinely coupled cognitive system rather than a tool-use relationship, directly reinforcing the "extension of my brain" framing from the first research pass. It is conceptual rather than mathematical, and stands in real tension with the representational, parameter-based frameworks that make up most of the rest of this report — enactivism partly rejects the assumption that cognition consists of manipulable internal representations — a tension worth naming rather than papering over, since this project pragmatically wants to use both.

**Global Workspace Theory.** Baars's (1988) original proposal and Dehaene and Changeux's Global Neuronal Workspace elaboration hold that specialized parallel processes compete for access to a limited-capacity workspace, and the winning content is broadcast globally, becoming available for report, memory, and flexible use — with Dehaene's taxonomy further distinguishing unconscious processing from global access from full metacognitive self-monitoring. This is a live and contested area of consciousness science more broadly, so it should be treated as one contender, not settled fact. For the project: mainly useful as a model of what wins the competition for the person's attention in the moment — connecting naturally to the attention-residue and interruption-cost findings from the first research pass — rather than as a source of any individual parameter.

### VI. Communication Design: Rational Speech Act Theory

Both research passes, working independently, arrived at the same framework for modeling how the AI should phrase a cue — worth flagging as a real convergence rather than a coincidence. Frank and Goodman's (2012, *Science*) Rational Speech Act theory formalizes Gricean pragmatics as recursive Bayesian inference: a pragmatic listener reasons about a pragmatic speaker who chooses utterances balancing informativeness against cost, with a literal listener sitting underneath both as the base case. Goodman and Stuhlmüller's (2013) extension shows specifically that a partial or under-informative utterance causes the listener to actively infer the fuller intended meaning precisely when the speaker is understood to have incomplete knowledge, and Degen's (2023, *Annual Review of Linguistics*) review confirms this general approach now handles implicature, reference, vagueness, and hyperbole successfully as a mature research program. This gives a second, independent theoretical justification — alongside the attention-residue and Zeigarnik-effect account already established — for exactly the same design choice: a deliberately partial, well-chosen cue doesn't just create a motivational pull toward the missing piece, it also engages the listener's own inferential machinery to reconstruct the fuller meaning themselves. Practically, cues should be designed to be pragmatically minimal-but-informative given a working model of what the person already knows and assumes, rather than simply short.

### VII. Building the Individual Model: Elicitation & Representation

**Personal Construct Theory and the Repertory Grid.** George Kelly's (1955) Personal Construct Theory holds that people understand the world through their own idiosyncratic system of bipolar constructs — "analytical–emotional," "safe–risky," and the like — which they use to anticipate events: an explicitly idiographic, constructivist theory built on a "person as scientist" metaphor. Its formal elicitation instrument, the Repertory Grid, presents triads of elements (people, situations, options, projects) and asks how two are alike and differ from the third, surfacing the constructs the person actually uses with minimal imposition from the researcher's own categories; the resulting grid supports formal analysis (multidimensional scaling, cluster analysis, formal concept analysis) and even quantifiable measures like cognitive complexity. This is the one genuinely concrete, purpose-built methodology in the entire landscape surveyed across both passes for eliciting a specific individual's own cognitive structure, rather than scoring them against someone else's pre-built inventory — a natural fit for this project's idiographic goal, and directly adaptable: a "virtual repertory grid" run over recurring elements already present in the user's transcripts (past decisions, projects, ideas, collaborators) could surface the actual bipolar dimensions along which the user construes their own work, handed to the AI as elicited structure rather than externally imposed labels. Given that it minimizes the risk of imposing categories the person wouldn't recognize as their own, this is worth piloting directly.

**Network psychometrics.** An alternative, complementary representational choice comes from network psychometrics (Borsboom & Cramer; van der Maas et al., 2006): rather than treating psychological constructs as the downstream effects of a single latent variable, this approach models them as networks of mutually reinforcing observable components connected by direct relationships, estimated via Ising models for binary data or Gaussian graphical models for continuous data (both Markov random fields), with a formal equivalence shown between Ising-model and item-response-theory approaches (Marsman et al., 2018). The field is growing quickly but comes with real cautions about the sample sizes needed to estimate high-dimensional networks reliably, and increasingly moves toward genuinely idiographic, single-subject time-series networks rather than purely cross-sectional, population-level ones. For the project: this offers a genuinely different representational option to the Repertory Grid — instead of scoring the person on independent trait-like dimensions, represent specific reasoning tendencies (seeks disconfirming evidence, defers decisions, reframes the problem before solving it) as nodes in a weighted network, capturing how activating one tendency tends to activate or suppress another. More data-hungry than the Repertory Grid, and edges should be presented as tentative rather than fixed, but it's a valuable complementary lens, particularly for capturing interactions between reasoning tendencies that an independent-trait model would miss.

### VIII. Curiosity & Learning-Progress Models

Oudeyer and Kaplan's developmental-robotics tradition and Schmidhuber's compression-progress account converge on the learning-progress hypothesis: agents are intrinsically rewarded not by novelty for its own sake but by the rate of improvement in their own predictions, which naturally steers exploration away from both the already-predictable and the hopelessly unlearnable, toward a productive middle zone. Gottlieb, Oudeyer and Lopes (2016, *Progress in Brain Research*) lay out this account alongside a competing information-gain/epistemic-value formulation, and Ten, Kaushik, Oudeyer and Gottlieb's (2021, *Nature Communications*) empirical work found that models incorporating a learning-progress term provide the best fit to how people actually allocate their time across multiple possible learning activities when left to explore freely. For the project: this is the clearest justification yet for the "let curiosity do the rest" design intuition running through this project — a well-placed minimal cue works not simply because it creates an itch or invites inference, but because it can be aimed specifically at the person's current zone of maximal learning progress: a gap that's genuinely closable given what they already know, rather than a triviality or an intractable tangent. The counterweight's targeting problem, in this vocabulary, is finding that zone for the specific person and topic at hand.

### IX. Why a Different Thinker Beats a Smarter Clone: Cognitive Diversity

Hong and Page's (2004, *PNAS*) "diversity trumps ability" result showed, under specified conditions, that a diverse group of good-enough problem-solvers can outperform a group assembled purely from the highest-ability individuals available. This is a live mathematical dispute, not settled fact: Thompson's (2014, *Notices of the American Mathematical Society*) critique argued the underlying theorem lacks real mathematical content or practical applicability and called the supporting simulation contrived, and subsequent replies — Page's own 2015 response, later work by Kuehn, Singer, and Grim and colleagues in 2019 — defend the result but only under fairly strict conditions on group size, population size, and how diversity and problem difficulty are defined. For the project: this is conceptually useful as a justification for the AI being a genuinely differently-structured collaborator — the value of the counterweight comes from bringing a different heuristic set to bear, not from being a higher-ability version of the same thinking style — but it's a group-level result with only a loose connection to an individual dyad, and the live critique means it should be cited as a bounded, conditional finding rather than a general law.

### X. Reinforcing the Scope Boundary: Personal Identity & Psychological Continuity

Derek Parfit's (1984, *Reasons and Persons*) work on personal identity argues that what actually matters in questions of survival and continuity isn't strict numerical identity, but psychological connectedness and continuity — a relation defined over chains of memory, intention, belief, and character, which can in principle hold to different degrees and can come apart from questions of a single persisting self, as his teletransportation and fission thought experiments are designed to show. This remains a canonical, still-live reference point in personal-identity debates. For the project: deliberately brief, since identity and values are explicitly out of scope, but genuinely useful for reinforcing exactly where the scope boundary sits — "preserving how someone reasons" is a claim about the functional or psychological continuity of a reasoning pattern, in Parfit's sense, and explicitly not a claim about preserving or modeling the person's self or identity. This gives the "how you think, not who you are" distinction established earlier in this project a precise philosophical anchor.

### XI. The Empirical Reality Check: Does This Actually Work From Text?

This is the question none of the mathematical or theoretical elegance above can answer by itself, and it's worth stating plainly before any of this gets built into a prompt.

**The strongest positive result, and its real limits.** Park, Zou, Shaw, Hill, Cai, Morris, Willer, Liang and Bernstein's 2024 study built individual generative agents for 1,052 real people by injecting each person's own two-hour interview transcript directly into an LLM's context, with no fine-tuning involved. The resulting agents reproduced each person's own answers on the General Social Survey about 85% as accurately (normalized) as that same person reproduced their own answers two weeks later, and did almost as well on Big Five personality measures — but dropped to roughly the accuracy of simple demographic baselines on economic games, meaning the rich interview text added little when it came to predicting strategic decisions specifically. This is a genuinely strong validation of the overall method this project is built on — rich individual text fed as context to an LLM, rather than fine-tuning or hand-built parameters — while simultaneously showing exactly where that method's reach currently ends: attitudes and self-reported traits, yes; strategic and economic reasoning, not so much.

**A complementary population-level result.** The Centaur foundation model (Binz, Akata, Bethge and colleagues, published in *Nature* in 2025) fine-tuned a large open model on a dataset covering more than ten million individual choices from over sixty thousand participants across 160 psychology experiments, and the resulting model out-predicts classical cognitive models on held-out participants and generalizes to entirely new experimental setups. This is impressive at predicting behavior in aggregate, but a 2026 critique cautions against reading genuine cognitive mechanism into how well the model fits — good behavioral prediction doesn't by itself validate any of the interpretable parameters this report has been describing.

**The validity concerns specific to individual "digital twins."** A growing body of recent work documents real instability in exactly this kind of text-derived individual modeling — what's been called "measurement phantoms": personality and theory-of-mind scores that swing noticeably under trivial rephrasing, and factor structures that don't hold up under standard psychometric scrutiny (2025 work). Construct-validity analyses through 2026 find these text-derived "twins" reasonably good at capturing broad semantic structure and general response profiles, but weak on item-level variance, known heuristic biases, staying grounded to a specific point in time, and measurement invariance — with documented risks of the model subtly displacing or distorting the very construct it's meant to represent, and of unintended confounding between what the model is asked about and what it actually reflects.

**The bottom line for the prompt.** No method exists today, validated or otherwise, for literally computing an evidence-accumulation rate, a reinforcement-learning rate, a metacognitive-efficiency ratio, or a precision-weighting parameter directly from someone's conversational text. Every parameter discussed in this report should be presented to the AI, and by the AI, as a qualitative, low-to-moderate-confidence hypothesis about the person — something to be stated, tested against how the person actually reacts, and revised — never as a number that's been measured.

## Recommendations

Combining both passes' recommendations into one staged build order:

**Stage 1 — Set the frame before anything else.** Declare the model computational/algorithmic in Marr's sense, ground the "skeleton wears different bodies" framing explicitly in functionalism and multiple realizability, and invoke the Good Regulator Theorem as the formal reason the model needs to exist at all. This prevents category errors downstream and gives the project a stated, defensible philosophical position rather than an implicit one.

**Stage 2 — Build the belief-and-decision spine around the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter, with evidence-accumulation, reinforcement learning, and metacognition as complementary parameters.** Structure the person-model around a small set of individually-varying, clearly-named parameters: belief-updating rate and assumed volatility per domain (HGF); decision caution and evidence-extraction efficiency (drift-diffusion); planning-versus-habit tendency and arbitration between them (model-based/model-free RL); and metacognitive calibration per domain (meta-d′/M-ratio). Treat every one of these as a qualitative tag, not a fitted number, and mark confidence accordingly — if transcript data is ever too sparse to distinguish two of these, collapse to a single qualitative description rather than force a false distinction.

**Stage 3 — Encode the two open design questions as explicit, checkable rules rather than vibes.** For interruption timing: implement something like the Expected Value of Control rule directly — cue only when the estimated value of redirecting attention exceeds the estimated disruption cost, which is exactly why a minimal hint should be preferred by default over a full explanation. For recognizing diminishing returns: implement a Marginal Value Theorem-style rate comparison, cross-checked against the documented human tendency to under-search — meaning the rule should be at least as ready to cue continued exploration as to cue stopping, shifting toward abandonment-only only if the person reacts poorly to "keep going" nudges in practice.

**Stage 4 — Make observation-versus-inference separation and confidence-tagging genuinely load-bearing, not decorative.** Every claim the AI makes about the person should be explicitly tagged as an observation (directly present in the transcripts) or an inference (model-derived), carry a stated confidence level, and name what would change that assessment if it turned out to be wrong — this last part directly inoculates against the unfalsifiability trap the Bayesian-brain and active-inference critiques describe, and should be treated as non-negotiable given how strongly and independently both research passes converged on this same caution.

**Stage 5 — Design the cue mechanism around the confirmed triple justification.** A hint should be small (Expected Value of Control: low disruption cost), incomplete in a way that creates a genuine attentional pull (attention residue / Zeigarnik effect from the first research pass), and phrased to invite the listener's own inference rather than deliver the full answer (Rational Speech Act theory) — and it should be aimed, when possible, at the person's actual zone of maximal learning progress rather than an arbitrary gap. Three independent frameworks converging on the same design is a strong basis for treating it as a default policy rather than a hunch.

**Stage 6 — Actually build the idiographic model using elicitation, not assumption.** Pilot a virtual repertory grid over recurring elements already present in the transcripts to surface the person's own bipolar construct dimensions, rather than scoring them against an externally-designed inventory; consider network-style representation as a complementary lens for capturing how reasoning tendencies interact, keeping any such edges explicitly tentative.

**Stage 7 — State the empirical ceiling in the prompt itself.** Tell the AI directly that text-derived individual modeling is validated at the level of general attitudes and self-described traits but not at the level of strategic or economic decision-making, and that every specific numeric-sounding parameter discussed above is a hypothesis to be tested against how the person actually responds, not a measurement — revising down when the person's real reactions don't match.

**Benchmarks that would justify revisiting this plan:**
- If a virtual repertory-grid pilot yields constructs the user immediately recognizes and endorses as genuinely theirs → promote it to the primary model-building method, ahead of the parameter-based frameworks.
- If the AI's qualitative HGF/DDM/RL/meta-d′-style characterizations correctly anticipate real reactions in practice → keep and refine them; if they consistently misfire → demote them to background framing rather than active predictions.
- If the person reacts negatively to "keep exploring" nudges more than to "you can stop now" nudges → shift the stopping-rule balance toward abandonment, overriding the general early-stopping-bias correction for this specific person.
- If validated methods for extracting any of these parameters from natural language actually emerge → upgrade the relevant parameter from qualitative to quantitative.
- If the model ever shows "measurement phantom" symptoms — characterizations that flip under simple rephrasing, or that the user actively disowns — tighten back toward user-elicited constructs and away from any imposed framework-based inventory.

## Caveats

- **Contested status is the rule here, not the exception, and both passes reached this conclusion independently.** The Bayesian brain and predictive processing face real unfalsifiability, intractability, and biological-plausibility critiques; strict two-system dual-process theory is challenged by serious single-process accounts; the dynamical-systems hypothesis has been self-critiqued as under-specified; and even Hong & Page's cognitive-diversity theorem has a live, unresolved mathematical dispute. Nothing in this report should be presented to or by the AI as settled consensus.
- **No parameter here can currently be computed from free text, only analogized to it.** Drift rate, boundary separation, learning rate, volatility, meta-d′, and precision-weighting were all validated on controlled behavioral tasks with many repeated trials, not on conversational transcripts. Every use of this vocabulary in the eventual prompt should be flagged as a qualitative, revisable hypothesis, never a fitted measurement.
- **The idiographic/ergodicity caution from the very first research pass still governs everything here.** Findings about how these parameters behave across populations (e.g., drift rate as a stable trait factor) don't guarantee that any specific parameter is stable within this one person over time or across different domains of their life; the model should stay explicitly provisional and open to revision.
- **A handful of citations deserve verification against original print sources before anything here is treated as publication-grade** — specifically Simon's exact 1956 wording, the precise page reference for Parfit's account of Relation R, and the exact venue for a 2025 critique of the Bayesian-brain hypothesis, whose DOI resolved oddly during one of the two research sessions.
- **Source quality is uneven but appropriately weighted.** Both passes leaned primarily on peer-reviewed primary literature (venues including *Neuron*, *Psychological Review*, *Science*, *Nature*, *Frontiers*, *eLife*, various *Annual Reviews*, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, *Nature Communications*, *Scientific Reports*, *PNAS*); a small number of framing points were drawn from lower-authority secondary sources and were used only for orientation, never as load-bearing evidence for a specific claim.
- **This document synthesizes two separate research passes rather than commissioning new research to arbitrate between them.** Where the two passes' details differed slightly on the same framework, the more specific and recently-sourced version was kept; no genuine contradictions between the two passes turned up, only differences in coverage depth.
