# IDREA Corrected Operating Sequence

## Purpose

This sequence is the project-specific application of the Universal Virtual Enterprise workflow. It corrects the failure observed in the referenced task, where the work jumped from literal requirements to adhesion mechanisms and then to cross-domain dependencies before completing problem reconstruction and evidence-led landscape mapping.

## Stage 0 — Accept and frame the supplied challenge

Preserve the supplied input: design a battery-powered robotic prototype for stable multi-axis traversal across vertical and non-planar surfaces. Record the immediate deliverables, deadline, funding situation, intended maturity, quality benchmark, and what is deliberately not being optimised.

At this stage, do not reinterpret the challenge away. “Build a rover” or “build this robot” may be the correct project starting point. The enterprise must preserve the input while making its hidden structure explicit.

## Stage 1 — Establish the literal fact base

Extract every explicit requirement, evaluation metric, constraint, failure condition, round requirement, and submission rule from the official challenge page and rulebook. Record exact wording, page/section, source URL, retrieval date, and evidence state.

Separate official requirements from user-stated constraints, assistant interpretations, engineering implications, hypotheses, estimates, and open questions. Resolve contradictions or preserve them in an uncertainty register; never silently choose one interpretation.

## Stage 2 — Reconstruct the actual problem and operating context

Ask what the supplied challenge is trying to make teams demonstrate, what kinds of real-world operations it represents, who would use or be affected by such a robot, what environments and surfaces matter, what failure consequences exist, and what the competition evaluator can actually observe.

This stage is research-led. The project must not state that rope access is dangerous, that current robotic alternatives are siloed, that specific surfaces are common, or that a particular adhesion method fails in a particular way until those claims are supported by appropriate evidence.

The output is a researched problem model and operating context, not a selected mechanism.

## Stage 3 — Map the landscapes

Map, as relevant:

- Problem and stakeholder landscape.
- Industrial operating environments.
- Surface/material/geometry landscape.
- Existing inspection, maintenance, climbing, and automation systems.
- Adhesion and locomotion technology landscape.
- Power, battery, controls, sensing, and embedded-computing landscape.
- Safety, failsafe, operator, and recovery landscape.
- Evaluation and simulation landscape.
- Manufacturing, sourcing, cost, and prototype landscape.
- Standards, regulations, venue, and competition landscape.

Each landscape should contain sources, knowns, unknowns, contradictions, relevance, freshness, and architecture-changing implications.

## Stage 4 — Build the factor, evidence, and dependency catalogue

For every factor, record its domain, definition, current state, affected requirement or decision, consequence if unresolved, evidence needed, current evidence state, dependencies, tradeoffs, owner, and revisit trigger.

The catalogue must include, at minimum, mass, payload, adhesion force, friction, shear, torque, curvature, surface roughness, coatings, contamination, water/moisture, magnetic properties, geometry transitions, actuator force, power draw, battery energy, thermal behaviour, control latency, sensing, communication loss, power loss, recovery, surface damage, operator safety, testing, manufacturing, cost, and evaluation repeatability—but this list is only a starting hypothesis. Research determines whether additional factors are required.

## Stage 5 — Derive requirements and success conditions

Translate the literal challenge into verifiable system requirements without inventing unsupported requirements. Define measurable success conditions for each motion mode, surface class, payload condition, runtime condition, control mode, failure mode, and safety behaviour.

Each requirement must have a source, rationale, owner, priority, verification method, acceptance threshold, uncertainty, dependencies, and change history.

## Stage 6 — Generate and compare architecture families

Only after the factor catalogue is sufficiently mature should candidate architectures be generated and compared. Candidate families may include the inherited suction/airflow, multi-modal, and soft/reconfigurable concepts, as well as alternatives discovered through research.

The comparison must cover system-level behaviour, not only adhesion. It must include chassis, locomotion, power, mass, control, sensing, safety, failsafe, surface interaction, curvature transition, manufacturability, cost, testability, operating concept, and evidence burden.

Preserve viable rejected alternatives. A rejected option is not necessarily bad; it may be dominated only under the current constraints and evidence state.

## Stage 7 — Select a coherent system direction

Create a structured decision record containing alternatives, criteria, weights or priorities where justified, evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, dissent, selected direction, rejected paths, dependencies, consequences, conditions, and revisit triggers.

The decision is not final merely because one architecture scores highest in a matrix. It must be coherent across the whole dependency graph and must not rely on unretired architecture-changing unknowns.

## Stage 8 — Produce the canonical technical concept

Create the complete concept before making the presentation. It should include the mission/problem model, ConOps, system architecture, subsystem decomposition, interfaces, physics, mass and power budgets, control architecture, safety and recovery, manufacturing path, test strategy, simulation or mathematical validation plan, limitations, prototype roadmap, and evidence boundary.

The abstract and ten-slide presentation are derived views. They compress the canonical concept; they do not substitute for it.

## Stage 9 — Attack and validate

Attempt to break the selected concept through calculations, sensitivity analysis, failure modes, adverse surfaces, payload variations, curvature variations, power/communication loss, sensor errors, control delays, contamination, manufacturing tolerances, and operator mistakes.

Where physical simulation or testing is unavailable, label the result as a design calculation or hypothesis. Do not present mathematical plausibility as physical validation.

## Stage 10 — Compress and deliver

Only after the canonical body of work and evidence boundary are stable should the project produce the Round 1 abstract and presentation. Each claim in the submission must trace to a source, calculation, test, or explicitly labelled design inference.

## Non-negotiable behaviour rules

1. Do not jump from a rulebook requirement to a mechanism.
2. Do not treat assistant-generated context as researched fact.
3. Do not update continuity after every conversational turn; update after a substantive phase or concrete decision.
4. Do not allow one agent or model to stand in for an entire specialist capability when the problem requires a team.
5. Do not compress disagreement into false consensus.
6. Do not select an architecture before identifying the factors that could overturn it.
7. Do not call a calculation, diagram, or simulation substitute real-world validation.
8. Do not create the submission artefacts before the canonical technical body of work.
9. Preserve rejected alternatives, negative evidence, unresolved questions, and limitations.
10. Return upward whenever implementation, research, or verification contradicts the current problem model or architecture.

## Current gate

The project is currently between Stage 1 and Stage 3. The immediate objective is to establish the researched problem/context model and decision-space map. Architecture selection is not yet authorised.
